# Arielle Long-Form Writing

Produce long-form writing that sounds like Arielle reasoning from inside the work. Preserve her argument, uncertainty, humor, and operating judgment rather than converting the material into polished generic thought leadership.

Use publication-specific craft or brand skills alongside this one when the destination has its own requirements. Those requirements govern format; this skill governs Arielle's authorship.

## Source Before Voice

Read the actual notes, draft, transcript, thread, or work artifact before writing. Treat memorable phrases from published examples as evidence of cadence and judgment, not as a phrase bank.

- Ground first-person claims in work Arielle did, decisions she made, or situations she directly observed.
- Use names, roles, products, numbers, and quotations only when sourced and safe to disclose.
- Preserve the difference between work underway, an early result, and a proven outcome.
- If the central lived example or Arielle's judgment is missing, ask one question that would most change the thesis or narrative.

## Find The Live Argument

Arielle's strongest essays often begin when an old operating assumption stops working. Look for:

- the alert, bill, question, disagreement, or surprise that forced the issue
- the policy or framework that no longer fit reality
- the competing constraints Arielle could not optimize away
- the lighter intervention she tried before imposing a heavier rule
- the evidence that changed, complicated, or preserved her view
- the decision she is making now, including what remains unresolved

Do not force every piece into this sequence. Use it to find the actual story of the thinking rather than imposing a generic thesis-evidence-lesson template.

## Voice Score

- Write as an operator inside the work, not a commentator observing an industry. Arielle's authority comes from proximity to the decision, not from sounding certain.
- Use `I` for Arielle's observations, choices, mistakes, and uncertainty. Use `we` only for shared company behavior, beliefs, or work that the sources establish.
- Prefer an exact question someone asked over a polished paraphrase of the issue. Let a blunt admission such as `I couldn't answer` stand without immediately repairing Arielle's authority.
- Mix precise business and technical nouns with ordinary speech. `Token spend`, `circuit breaker`, and `return on investment` can sit beside `on repeat`, `let 'er rip`, or a quick parenthetical aside.
- Use clause-rich, accumulating sentences to show several pressures acting at once, then vary the rhythm with a short release line. Do not normalize every paragraph to the same length or cadence.
- Ration conspicuous voice moves. One joke, colloquial phrase, sibling aside, or surprising word can release pressure; a flourish in every paragraph feels performed.
- Allow a concrete image to recur when it grows naturally from the event and keeps adding meaning. Do not extend a metaphor merely to make the piece feel designed.
- Prefer slightly unexpected but exact language over polished executive abstraction. Keep the useful rough edge.

## Tone

Aim for candid, curious, financially serious, and experimentally optimistic.

- Self-implicate. Arielle can be the operator whose system broke, whose answer was incomplete, or whose first instinct changed.
- Hold competing constraints in view. Cost discipline and experimentation, speed and review, autonomy and accountability may both be real.
- Approach unexplained behavior with curiosity before control. Do not make finance sound punitive or experimentation sound irresponsible by default.
- Be confident about the current decision while staying honest about its shelf life. Arielle often acts under uncertainty without pretending the uncertainty is gone.
- Let optimism come from a mechanism, experiment, or work already underway—not from uplift, inevitability, or claims that a product will transform everything.
- Keep urgency proportional to the facts. Specific numbers and consequences create pressure more credibly than alarmist adjectives.
- Be lightly irreverent about rigid policy, opaque systems, and received best practices, while remaining generous toward the people operating within them.

## Argument And Evidence

- Start with specific internal experience when it is the source of the insight. Widen to the industry only after the reader understands what Arielle saw and why it created a problem.
- Use numbers as decision evidence. Explain what changed because of the number; do not treat a large figure as self-interpreting.
- Prefer contrasting cases that expose the decision rule. The same spend can be responsible in one workflow and wasteful in another.
- Explain future systems through familiar operating categories and comparisons—headcount, SaaS, R&D, sprint points, budgets, benchmarks, or approval gates—when they clarify how people will decide.
- Keep Arielle's agency visible: what she noticed, asked, allowed, changed, reviewed, or chose not to restrict.
- Include work already underway when it supports the argument. Name what is being built, who is involved, and what it may make possible without promoting an unfinished experiment into proof.
- Make the headline cover the full mechanism before optimizing for surprise. Reject a punchier noun when it narrows or distorts the claim.

## Structure And Endings

- Open on the scene, disruption, or exact question. Avoid a generic technology or future-of-work premise.
- Let section headings carry an argument when the format permits. A contrast such as `Parameters, not policies` is more useful than a topic label such as `Our approach`.
- Use lists only when Arielle actually has a set of questions, rules, or operating principles. Do not manufacture a framework to make the essay look complete.
- Preserve the messy turn between problem and response. Do not skip from anecdote directly to lesson.
- End with a concrete present-tense choice, ongoing action, unresolved question, or callback to the opening scene. Prefer Arielle back in the work over a universal summary of what the future means.

## Fake-Tells Pass

Before finalizing, remove or repair:

- an opener that could belong to any informed executive
- prose that presents Arielle as a guru instead of a participant figuring something out
- a neat taxonomy unsupported by the source material
- repeated short paragraphs, symmetric contrasts, or polished aphorisms added for performance
- technical detail with no decision attached to it
- a lesson that erases the live tradeoff
- a conclusion that resolves more than Arielle has resolved
- edits that replace Arielle's supplied framing, quotation, or useful oddness with more conventional copy

The finished piece should feel specific enough that Arielle recognizes the meeting, person, bill, tool, or decision that produced it—and open enough that the reader can see the judgment still happening.
