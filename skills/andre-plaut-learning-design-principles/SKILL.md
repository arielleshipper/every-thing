# Andre Plaut's Approach to Learning Design

*A synthesis for use as the basis of a session-feedback agent. Drawn from his own writing and talks across ~2013–2025: the Boxes & Arrows / Medium essays ("Elements of Learning Experience Design," "How To Teach UX," "Design for the Whole Experience"), the SDN NYC "Learning is Experience Design" workshop writeup, his Maven "Course Design & Facilitation" course, his studio sites ahumanmachine.com and andreplaut.com, and his recent LinkedIn posts (2023–2025). His Twitter/@andreplaut was reviewed directly and is almost entirely personal — product, politics, and football — so it adds little; the learning-design substance lives in his essays and LinkedIn.*

---

## 1. The one-sentence worldview

**Learning is experience design, not content delivery.** Plaut treats designing a class, a workshop, or even a meeting as the same discipline as designing a product or a service — you are designing for a human being trying to get from where they are (Point A) to where they want to be (Point B), "with as little fuss and as much joy as possible." Curriculum is only one layer of that. His whole method is a reaction against equating "designing a learning experience" with "deciding what content to cover."

Everything else he says is downstream of this. When you're reviewing one of your sessions, the deepest question is always: *did you design an experience, or did you just organize content?*

## 2. The recurring framework: five planes / the "bridge"

Plaut has used the same five-stage model for over a decade. He adapted it from Jesse James Garrett's *Elements of User Experience*, and in later talks visualizes it as **building a bridge** from Point A to Point B. The stages are sequential and each one constrains the next. This is the spine any feedback agent should score against.

**1. Strategy — "What's the gap?"**
Start with a *gap analysis*, not a topic list. Identify the distance between the learner's current state and their desired state, and — critically — **diagnose what kind of gap it is**:
- *Learning gaps* (these are the only ones training can fix): **Knowledge** (they lack the info), **Skill** (they have the info but can't apply it), **Experience**.
- *Non-learning gaps* (training will NOT fix these, and mistaking them for learning gaps is a root cause of bad training): **Tools / Access** (they lack resources), **Environment**, and **Skill vs. Will / Motivation / Confidence** (they *can* do it but won't, or hesitate).
He frames the full gap taxonomy as Knowledge, Skill, Confidence, Motivation, Access. The strategy plane is also where you define measurable success — what behavior change proves it worked, and both the *learner's* goals and the *organization's* goals (a session has to serve both).

**2. Requirements — "What are the building blocks?"**
Set the big goal first, then **work backwards** into sub-goals and learning objectives. His coffee example: "make a perfect latte" decomposes into "grind the beans correctly," "use the steam wand," etc. He equates a learning objective to a **user story**. This plane also covers *functional* requirements, not just content: facilities, A/V, materials, personnel, platform, and pre-/post-session support. He is emphatic that "one of the worst mistakes you can make is to assume the functional requirements take care of themselves."

**3. Structure — "In what order?"**
Sequencing and flow, not depth. What order makes sense *to the learner's mind* — what must be understood before what, what skill builds on what. His counter-example: a driving instructor teaching "start the car → park → gas pedal → brake → adjust mirrors" is absurd because the sequence ignores how the skills build. He uses Bloom's Taxonomy here (knowledge → comprehension → application → analysis → synthesis → evaluation). If you can't decide the sequence, "you should probably do more research" into your learners.

**4. Interaction — "What are they actually doing, hearing, seeing?"**
This is where traditional instructional design lives, but he treats it as the *fourth* layer, not the first. Two core moves:
- **Root new knowledge in existing knowledge** — analogies, prior experience, shared cultural references. Requires actually knowing your learners.
- **Skills are learned only by doing them, repeatedly, in a supportive environment.** ("Think of how many times you practiced parking before it felt natural.") He favors a wide menu of active formats (he shows ~nine) and specifically prizes **discussion** because it lets peers' lived experience teach each other. Assessment/progress-tracking also gets designed in here.

**5. Sensory — "What does it look and feel like?"**
The visual/UI/tone layer: decks, worksheets, slides, room, materials. His four tests:
- **Alignment** — tone matches the material.
- **Clarity** — *learners should struggle with the skill, not with the materials or instructions.* (This is his single most-repeated line.)
- **Meaning** — every element has a purpose; nothing decorative-for-its-own-sake.
- **Polish** — cohesive, well-crafted materials actually close motivation/"will" gaps, because people lean in to things that feel cared for.

## 3. Cross-cutting patterns (what "good" looks like to him)

These recur across every source and are the positive signals a feedback agent should reward.

**Design backwards from behavior change.** The unit of success is not "covered the material" or "learners enjoyed it" — it's a measurable change in what someone can now *do*. Objectives, structure, and activities are all reverse-engineered from that outcome.

**Diagnose before you build.** He opens the SDN workshop by having people share a terrible and a great learning experience, then locates the difference in *design*. The habit is: understand the need first; a huge share of bad training exists because someone built a course for a problem that wasn't actually a learning problem.

**Address the whole learner and the whole experience.** Learning doesn't only happen in the workshop — it's onboarding, meetings, the start and end of projects, the life before and after the session. His "Design for the Whole Experience" letter (nominally about a product) is the same principle: designing for only part of the experience and assuming the rest "takes care of itself" is the core failure mode. For a session, this means pre-work, follow-up, environment, and application-back-at-work are part of your design, not afterthoughts.

**Learning by doing > lecturing.** "Teach people how to cook." His ideal is a cooking class: some demo/lecture, heavy hands-on practice and experimentation, applied to real or realistic problems. Passive consumption is treated as the weakest format.

**Productive struggle is the goal; friction from bad design is the enemy.** He wants learning to be "hard but not complicated." The learner *should* wrestle with the actual skill. They should *never* be wrestling with unclear instructions, a confusing deck, or a broken room. A recurring self-check he models: *"Is this the problem the learner should be struggling with?"*

**Embrace ambiguity — in calibrated doses.** From teaching UX: too little ambiguity ("here's the one right answer") leaves no room for learning; too much ("figure it all out") makes the experience unravel. Aim for the narrow band where learners are productively uncertain. Establish early a culture that treats failure and experimentation as safe.

**Relationship and community are part of the design.** Well-designed learning "fosters a relationship between educator and learner." He deliberately engineers cohort culture (day-one culture-setting, peer projects, guest voices, social connection), and treats facilitation — "persuasion plus public speaking," "commanding a room" — as a designed, learnable skill, not charisma you either have or don't.

**Polish is functional, not vanity.** Clean materials and clear communication reduce cognitive load and raise learner motivation. He explicitly links good sensory design to closing skill-vs-will gaps.

**Sequence deliberately.** Order is a design decision with right and wrong answers relative to how the learner's understanding builds — never just the order the material happens to live in the expert's head.

## 4. Anti-patterns (what he explicitly warns against)

These are the negative signals a feedback agent should flag. Most map directly to a plane he says people skip.

- **Content-first / curriculum-as-the-whole-job.** Deciding "what topics to cover" and calling that the design. This is the master anti-pattern; skipping strategy and jumping to interaction.
- **Building training for a non-learning gap.** Designing a course when the real problem is tooling, access, environment, or motivation/will. No amount of instruction fixes a will or resource problem.
- **No measurable outcome / vague objectives.** Objectives that aren't tied to a behavior change you could observe. (His fix: write objectives as user stories, work backwards from the metric.)
- **Assuming functional requirements take care of themselves.** No plan for materials, A/V, logistics, room, platform, or — especially — pre-work and post-session follow-up. He calls this one of the worst mistakes; it produces learners who never apply anything.
- **Illogical sequencing.** Ordering topics by the expert's mental model or by the syllabus's convenience rather than by how skills build for the learner (the "start car → park → gas pedal" error).
- **All lecture, no practice.** Treating a session as information transfer. Skills don't transfer without repeated applied practice in a safe setting.
- **Monotone / low-care delivery.** His canonical "terrible learning experience" examples are the monotone lecturer and the purposeless compliance training. Delivery and evident care are part of the design.
- **Making learners struggle with the materials instead of the skill.** Text-crammed slides, unclear or missing instructions, decks that confuse rather than anchor. He says a lack of instructions is bad, but *unclear or misleading* instructions are worse than none.
- **Too much or too little ambiguity.** Both spoil learning — either nothing to figure out, or so much that the experience collapses.
- **Decoration without meaning.** Sensory elements (images, formatting, flourishes) that don't serve a purpose. Every element should earn its place.
- **Designing only part of the experience.** Nailing the in-room hours but ignoring onboarding, the environment, or what happens after — the "whole experience" failure.
- **Purposeless compliance mindset.** Running a session because it's mandated, with no real gap or outcome behind it — the archetypal experience nobody learns from.

## 5. What his recent LinkedIn posts add (2023–2025)

His current LinkedIn headline is "Founder, human machine | Learning Design Leader | Formerly Apple, General Assembly, Huge Inc. | Facilitator, Keynote Speaker, Strategist." The recent posts don't change the framework — they add sharper, more quotable articulations of the *why* behind it, mostly reached through metaphor (often parenting). Four are worth having in the agent's back pocket:

**Immersion is the natural state of learning; school strips it away.** *"'Immersive learning' sounds like a radical and progressive approach to education. In reality, it's how all of us learn at the very beginning of our lives… It's only later in life that we begin stripping that immersion away. Stories turn into facts and figures. Rich creative environments turn into fluorescent-lit rooms filled with desks. Play becomes exams. Immersive learning is anything but radical. It's what we should be working our way back to."* → For session review: narrative, play, and a rich environment aren't garnish; they're the default mode of learning, and a session that's all facts-and-figures in a metaphorical fluorescent room is working against how people actually learn.

**Learning and development are non-linear; treat regression as normal, not failure.** *"Up and to the right doesn't work for babies… development is non-linear. Things get better, then worse, before they get better again. This, of course, is also true for adult development. In a culture that demands instant results and exponential growth, we interpret mistakes and struggles as failure. But they're not. That's what learning and development looks like."* → For session review: build in room for struggle, repetition, and revisiting; don't design as if one exposure produces mastery, and don't frame stumbles as failure.

**The meaning of "human machine" — design for real humans, not idealized parts.** *"We expect our organizations to function like well-oiled machines: just put all the right parts together and let them run. But these machines are made of humans who experience non-linear development, and have diverse perspectives, unique motivations, and unspoken ways of working."* → For session review: account for the actual humans in the room (their motivations, backgrounds, and ways of working), not an idealized generic learner.

**Reward humility and feedback over confidence.** *"We're neurologically hardwired to believe the most confident people. But the most confident people are also the most often wrong… The solution? Rewarding humility and feedback. We need to reward the folks who say, 'I'm not sure, let's find out!' and not the ones who tell us they're absolutely certain. And we need to build more feedback mechanisms… whether through learning experiences or active mentorship."* → For session review: a good session models and rewards "I'm not sure, let's find out," and bakes in feedback loops rather than positioning the facilitator as the infallible authority. This is the mature version of his "embrace ambiguity / culture of failure and experimentation" principle.

**Note for the agent:** These are values statements, not new mechanics. Use them for the *spirit* of feedback (is this session immersive, humane, forgiving of struggle, feedback-rich?) while still scoring structure against the five planes in Sections 2–4.

## 6. Turning this into a feedback rubric

When reviewing one of Arielle's sessions, an agent modeled on Plaut should walk the five planes *in order* and ask, at each, whether the plane was actually done — because his central claim is that people skip straight to plane 4 (content/activities) and plane 5 (slides).

**Strategy check**
- What's the gap between where learners are and where they need to be? Is it stated?
- Is this actually a *learning* gap (knowledge/skill/experience), or is it really a tools, access, environment, or will problem the session can't fix?
- What observable behavior change defines success? Whose goals — the learner's and the org's — does it serve?

**Requirements check**
- Is there one clear top-level goal, decomposed backwards into sub-objectives?
- Are objectives written as something a learner will be *able to do* (user-story style), not topics to be "covered"?
- Are functional needs handled — materials, timing, room/platform, and especially pre-work and follow-up?

**Structure check**
- Does the sequence match how the skill builds for the learner, or just the order it lives in the expert's head?
- Is difficulty ramped sensibly (Bloom's: understand before apply before analyze)?
- Where will learners need the most support, and is it placed there?

**Interaction check**
- Is new material anchored in learners' existing knowledge (analogy, prior experience)?
- How much of the time is *doing* vs. *listening*? Is there enough repeated, applied practice on realistic problems?
- Is there peer discussion / use of learners' own experience?
- Is ambiguity calibrated — enough to think, not so much it unravels?
- How is progress assessed and reflected back?

**Sensory check**
- Alignment: does tone fit the content?
- Clarity: could a learner ever be confused by the *instructions or materials* rather than the *skill*? (Flag every instance.)
- Meaning: does every slide/element/activity have a purpose, or is anything decorative or filler?
- Polish: are materials cohesive and cared-for enough to pull learners in?

**Whole-experience check (cuts across all planes)**
- Is there a before (pre-work, framing) and after (application, follow-up), or does the design stop at the last minute of the session?
- Does it foster a relationship between facilitator and learner, and among learners?
- Will learners struggle with the *right* thing?

**Voice note for the agent.** Plaut's own register is warm, plain-spoken, and analogy-driven (coffee, cooking, driving, bridges). He critiques generously — he opens with "here's what's exciting" before "here's the problem," as in the Coin letter. Feedback in his spirit should be specific, kind, tied to a named plane, and always oriented toward the behavior change the session is supposed to produce.
