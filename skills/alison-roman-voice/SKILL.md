# Alison Roman: Voice & Craft Principles

A working guide to how Roman's writing operates across platforms, drawn from: her New York Times recipe columns and headnotes (2018-2020); her cookbooks (Dining In, Nothing Fancy, Sweet Enough, Something from Nothing); "A Newsletter" across several years and registers -- recipe-forward posts ("Home Movies Is Back," "Cooking On Vacation"), travel/recommendation posts ("I Went to Paris," "If You're Going to Maine"), business-announcement posts ("At Long Last, Some Very Exciting News," "I'm Going on a Book Tour," "Hosting Is Hard"), and overtly political/charitable posts (the June 2020 "tuna salad salad" newsletter, written in the direct aftermath of her controversy); her Home Movies YouTube intros; her 2020 Twitter apology statement; and profile/interview coverage in the New Yorker, Time, Jezebel, and Embedded (Substack).

Pulling from this range matters because Roman's voice isn't static -- it flexes based on stakes (a recipe intro vs. a public apology vs. a grief-adjacent newsletter) while keeping the same underlying mechanics. The guide below tries to name what stays constant across all of it.

## When to invoke

- Arielle asks to brainstorm a framing, opening, or structure "in the Roman mode" or references this guide directly.
- Arielle hands over a draft and asks for a pass, a read, or edits with this voice as the target -- especially for anything newsletter-shaped, first-person, or casual-register.
- A task calls for confident, funny, low-formality prose that still lands a real emotional or substantive point underneath the joke.
- Do NOT invoke silently on every piece of writing -- this is a specific register (chatty, bossy, self-implicating) that suits newsletters and personal essays, not formal or reported pieces. If unsure, ask.

## How to use this guide

### Brainstorming Mode

When there's no draft yet:

1. Identify the real subject and the real feeling underneath it -- Roman's pieces work because a low-stakes frame (what to cook, where to travel, a business update) sits on top of something she actually needs to say (fear, grief, defiance, tiredness, gratitude, accountability).
2. Propose 2-3 candidate openings using different techniques from Section 2 (direct-address bossy command, deadpan confession, mid-thought door-opener, or straight announcement) -- keep each to 1-2 sentences so Arielle can react fast.
3. For the one she picks, sketch the shape from Section 5: where's the bossy premise or plain announcement, where's the parenthetical aside that undercuts or complicates it, where does the real feeling surface, how does it close.
4. Surface a candidate closing line early (Section 6) -- Roman's endings are often a single deflating or tender sentence, and finding it early clarifies what the piece is actually for.
5. Ask, don't assume: confirm the real subject, the real feeling, and the stakes level (light recipe post vs. something with real accountability or grief in it) before drafting prose -- the register shifts noticeably by stakes (Section 10), and picking the wrong one first will read as tonally off no matter how well-executed the sentences are.

### Edit Pass Mode

When there's a draft:

1. Read the whole draft once before commenting.
2. Check it against this list and flag by section, don't silently rewrite:
   - Does the opening commit to a stance or announcement immediately, or does it hedge? (Section 2)
   - Are there parentheticals doing real work -- an aside, a joke, a retraction, a piece of withheld context -- or is the prose just declarative? (Section 3)
   - Is there at least one moment of direct address to the reader, giving permission, an order, or an inclusive "we"? (Section 4)
   - Is the self-deprecation specific and earned, or generic ("I'm not perfect, but...")? (Section 7)
   - Does a real feeling surface under the casual surface at least once, or does the piece stay entirely jokey? (Section 1)
   - Does the ending land as a single plain sentence, rather than trailing into summary? (Section 6)
   - If there's a joke, is it flagged with an exclamation point or "lol," or does it land on a flat, unmarked period? Unmarked is more her register. (Section 7a)
   - If the piece is handling something with real stakes (an apology, a loss, a serious announcement), does the register match Section 10's higher-stakes mode rather than staying purely jokey throughout?
3. Flag pastiche risk explicitly: Roman's voice reads as effortless because it's tightly edited chaos -- if a draft stacks run-ons, ellipses, and asides in every paragraph, it starts to feel like a tic rather than a style. Recommend reserving the loosest moves (ellipses, "etc.," trailing off, ALL CAPS emphasis) for one or two high-impact spots per piece.
4. Preserve Arielle's own sentences wherever the point already lands. This guide is for sharpening tone and permission-giving directness, not replacing her voice with Roman's.
5. Present suggestions as options with a one-line rationale each, not as a rewritten draft, unless she explicitly asks for a full rewrite pass.

A note on examples: quoted fragments from her work are kept deliberately minimal here. Every demonstration sentence below marked **[constructed]** is an original illustration of the technique, not her writing — some in her home domain (food/lifestyle), some transposed into ops, because the transposition is the point. For the real thing, read the pieces in the source list at the bottom; nothing substitutes for the originals.

## 1. The engine: low stakes, real feeling

Roman's default register is bossy-casual about small domestic things -- what to cook, what to buy, how to host without trying too hard, where to go on vacation. But the actual engine of her best writing is that a real feeling (fear, exhaustion, grief, defiance, gratitude) shows up underneath the low-stakes frame, usually announced almost in passing. Her postpartum newsletter opens on baby news delivered with the same energy as a recipe intro, then slides into real rawness ("nothing has ever seemed more sacred, more worthy of protection") without ever changing costume -- the sentence structure stays casual even when the content turns serious. Her travel-recommendations newsletter about Paris turns, mid-post, into a genuine piece of media criticism about the recommendation-industrial-complex on social platforms, delivered in exactly the same voice as the sponsor mention that preceded it. The feeling or the argument is smuggled in under cover of the mundane, not set off with a change in register.

**The rule:** never announce the emotional or intellectual shift with a tonal cue ("but in all seriousness"). Let the real feeling occupy the exact same conversational voice as the joke or the logistics before it.

**[constructed, lifestyle]** Anyway, the dog is fine, the dog is always fine, the dog is the only member of this household who has never once let me down, and if that sentence made you feel something, same.

**[constructed, ops]** We're doing okay over here. Payroll ran on time, nobody quit, and somewhere around week three of the reorg I stopped being able to sleep, which, fun, but that's a separate newsletter.

## 2. Openings: commit immediately

She almost never hedges an opening. Four characteristic entry points, drawn from across platforms:

**Bossy direct address.** A command or confident claim about what the reader should do, believe, or buy, delivered with zero preamble -- opening a biscuit-recipe post by telling you what you'd like to make this week, as settled fact rather than a suggestion.

**Deadpan confession.** Open on an admission that would normally require a defensive setup, and skip the setup entirely -- being "terrible at taking vacation," stated flatly, no qualifier.

**Mid-thought door-opener.** Open as if the reader already walked in partway through her sentence -- "Hello and welcome back (all of us)" -- the parenthetical immediately implicates the reader in whatever's been happening, without explaining what that is yet. She reuses this exact device across totally different contexts: welcoming readers back after maternity leave, and welcoming them back to a YouTube season after a hiatus, use nearly identical phrasing.

**Straight announcement, undercut by tone.** For business or career news, she'll open with the plain fact (a new book, a new store, a tour) delivered in a casual, almost breathless register that keeps the announcement from feeling like a press release -- excitement and self-awareness about the excitement, both at once.

**Public accountability, no evasion.** When the stakes are highest -- her 2020 apology -- she still opens by naming the fact plainly ("I've thought a lot this weekend about my interview and the things I said") rather than opening with a defense or a redirect. The commitment to directness doesn't disappear under pressure; if anything it tightens.

**[constructed, ops]** If you've been avoiding your expense report for six weeks, congratulations, you and I are now in a support group.

## 3. Parentheticals: the real workhorse

The single most load-bearing piece of Roman's punctuation is the parenthetical -- not a throwaway aside but frequently the place where the actual point, joke, retraction, or piece of context lives. In her postpartum post, two parentheticals stack back to back, the first supplying context the main sentence withheld, the second undercutting the vulnerability she just showed with a joke, in the same breath. In her apology tweet, a parenthetical does something different: it self-deprecates about her own writing habits ("succinctness has never been my strong suit") in the middle of a serious public statement, which somehow doesn't undercut the sincerity -- it signals that she's still herself even while being accountable. She also uses parentheticals for self-aware commentary on her own writing as she writes it, treating the reader as someone already fluent in the joke, so she doesn't have to finish it.

**[constructed, ops]** The vendor renewal came in 40% higher than last year (cool, normal, fine) and I spent a full day building a negotiation deck before remembering I could just... ask them why (revolutionary, I know).

## 4. Direct address and permission-giving

She talks to the reader like someone she already knows, and a huge share of her appeal is that she gives explicit permission to do the easier, less impressive thing, or invites the reader into an us. Her recommendation-newsletter writing does this differently than her recipe writing -- there, the "we" is about a shared, slightly embarrassing cultural compulsion (everyone wanting the exact same recommendations, the same precise Sézane shirt), and she includes herself in the compulsion even while naming it as a little strange. Across contexts, the move is the same: name the anxious or performative version of the thing, and grant an exemption or shared complicity, rather than a solution.

**[constructed, ops]** You do not need a twelve-slide deck to tell your team the roadmap changed. You need one Slack message and the nerve to send it before you talk yourself out of it.

## 5. Sentence rhythm: run-ons, then a flat landing

Her sentences frequently accumulate through comma splices and "and"-chains that mimic actual talking, landing on the clause that's the real point with no more emphasis than the clauses before it -- whether that's an admission of exhaustion tucked at the end of a list about baby gear, or (in her business-update newsletters) a long list of logistics and gratitude that ends on one small vulnerable admission about how hard the year actually was. The flatness of the landing is the joke and the vulnerability simultaneously; she never gives the punchline more punctuation than it needs.

**[constructed, food]** I made the sauce, burned the garlic, started over, burned it again, and served the pasta with olive oil and a lot of confidence.

## 6. Endings: one plain sentence, or a plain sign-off

Roman's pieces tend to close on a single short, declarative sentence that either deflates the buildup or quietly delivers the real point -- a plain mention of a recipe after several paragraphs of raw reflection, or, in more community-oriented posts, a simple "thank you for reading" that doesn't try to top what came before it. The whiplash between register (serious reflection, then a plain fact about dinner or a plain thank-you) is the closing device; it's not a joke exactly, it's a return to the mundane surface that signals *we're done being serious now, but I meant all of it.*

**[constructed, ops]** Anyway. The system works now. There's a Notion page.

## 7. Self-deprecation as armor, not apology

Her self-deprecation is specific and load-bearing, never generic. She doesn't say "I'm not perfect" -- she names the exact flaw with enough specificity that it reads as confession rather than false modesty: needing control while insisting she loves teaching; being "tired and sensitive"; not being able to write succinctly, even inside an apology for something serious. The specificity is what keeps it from reading as fishing for reassurance -- it's too particular to be anything but true. Crucially, the self-deprecation never undercuts her authority on the actual subject or her sincerity about the actual accountability; she can be scattered and imperfect and still mean every word of the apology, or still be right about the pasta.

**[constructed, ops]** I built the OKR process, I believe in the OKR process, and I have personally missed four of my own deadlines inside the OKR process, so.

## 7a. Dry wit: the unmarked joke

Distinct from both her ALL CAPS enthusiasm and her warm self-deprecation is a flatter, drier register: a joke delivered with zero punctuation asking the reader to notice it's a joke. No exclamation point, no "lol," no follow-up line acknowledging the bit landed. It's stated in exactly the same cadence as the sentence before and after it, and the flatness is the whole mechanism -- the reader has to catch it themselves.

A few real fragments (each under 15 words, one per source, kept short by design -- read the full pieces for the surrounding context):

- On the 2020 controversy, in her Time interview: she said she wasn't ashamed of who she was, and that she'd "f-cked up" -- no adjective softening it, no defensive clause attached.
- Deflecting the "is the stew a curry" criticism, per Jezebel: she described herself as coming from "no culture," calling herself "vaguely European" -- answering a real critique with a joke so flat it reads as almost bureaucratic.
- Describing what Instagram is actually for now, in her Embedded interview: she called it "basically a visual LinkedIn now" -- a flat redefinition stated as settled fact, no wind-up, no wink.
- In her 2020 apology tweet, mid-accountability, a dry aside about her own writing habits: "succinctness has never been my strong suit" -- funny and sincere in the same breath, with the joke doing zero work to soften the seriousness around it.
- In "hosting is hard," recounting a mid-fight moment with her husband over a Christmas tree, she closes the anecdote with a three-word quote and moves on without comment -- the joke is entirely in the abruptness of the cut, not in anything she adds after it.

**The rule for reuse:** dry wit only works if you resist the urge to underline it. If a sentence would normally end with an exclamation point or a "lol," try a period instead and see if the joke survives being taken completely seriously. If it does, that's the register. If it needs the exclamation point to read as a joke, it isn't dry wit -- it's just enthusiasm, which is a different (also valid, but separate) tool in her kit.

**[constructed, social/ops]** Told the team the migration would take "a few days." It has been eleven days. The migration continues.

**[constructed, social]** Bought a $40 candle to fix my personality. Reporting back in a week.

## 8. The "we"/community register (charity, politics, crisis)

In her most overtly political and charitable newsletter writing -- written in direct response to real-world crisis -- the register shifts noticeably from the recipe-newsletter default: more first-person plural, more direct instruction toward action (where to donate, what to read, who to support), less jokiness, though never entirely humorless. This is the clearest evidence that her voice is not one fixed setting but a range she moves across deliberately depending on what the moment calls for -- she doesn't force the usual bossy-charming register onto material that calls for something more sober, but she also doesn't abandon direct address and specificity; the same "tell you plainly what to do" energy that powers a recipe headnote powers a list of where to donate.

## 9. Vocabulary

**Casual intensifiers, stacked:** insanely, so good, really good, a lot, so much, wildly, extremely. She doesn't reach for a fancier word when a plain intensifier repeated does the job -- the flatness of the vocabulary is part of the bossy-confident tone; it doesn't need dressing up because the claim is not up for debate.

**Self-aware filler treated as punctuation:** "blah blah," "etc.," "and so on," "let's call it," "I guess," "or something," "which, fun." These aren't verbal tics to be edited out -- they're doing the work of a wink, signaling that she knows exactly how she sounds and is choosing to sound that way anyway.

**Domestic/bodily plain language:** milk-heavy, breast-focused, pump parts, toasted english muffins, broth out of a mug -- unglamorous, highly specific nouns delivered without any elevation, because elevating them would undercut the whole premise that the real, unglamorous version is the one worth writing about.

**Enthusiasm in caps and exclamation points, used sparingly and specifically:** LOVE, MORE, ALL CAPS on a single word inside an otherwise lowercase-energy sentence, deployed at the exact point of genuine excitement rather than scattered throughout for general emphasis.

**Business/gratitude vocabulary, kept plain:** "thank you," "so grateful," "I don't have more to share at the moment" -- even her professional announcements avoid corporate register; she narrates business milestones the same way she'd narrate a recipe, plainly and personally.

**Mild profanity for emphasis, sparingly:** used the way a friend would use it in conversation, not for shock -- it appears at the emotional peak of a sentence, not scattered throughout.

**Ellipses and trailing constructions:** "...etc.," "or something," "I don't know," "which, I guess" -- used to end a thought before it gets too earnest, a linguistic shrug that lets her have said the real thing without fully committing to having said it.

## 10. Range by stakes

The single biggest thing a one-newsletter sample misses: Roman's voice is not static. Reading across a recipe post, a travel post, a business-announcement post, a charity/politics post, and a public apology shows a consistent range:

- **Lowest stakes (a recipe intro, a "we're back" post):** maximum jokiness, ALL CAPS enthusiasm, parentheticals doing comic work almost exclusively.
- **Medium stakes (business news, travel, community updates):** jokiness stays, but gratitude and plain excitement take up more real estate; parentheticals start carrying real information as often as jokes.
- **Higher stakes (postpartum, grief-adjacent, hard year admissions):** the casual sentence structure and register stay exactly the same, but the content underneath gets genuinely raw; this is the mode covered most in Sections 1 and 6.
- **Highest stakes (public accountability):** direct address without evasion, self-deprecation still present but no longer doing comic work -- it signals humanness inside an otherwise serious statement, not deflection from it.

The craft lesson: the sentence-level mechanics (parentheticals, direct address, plain endings) stay constant across all four levels. What changes is only the ratio of joke to real content and how much room she gives the real content to sit unqualified.

## 11. What she never does

- Never explains the joke or softens the bossiness with a hedge ("I could be wrong, but...").
- Never elevates the register to signal that a moment is "serious" -- seriousness arrives in the same casual sentence structure as everything else, even in her highest-stakes writing.
- Never apologizes generically. Self-deprecation is always a specific, named flaw, even inside a real apology.
- Never lets a parenthetical go to waste -- it's always doing a joke, a retraction, or a piece of real information.
- Never ends on summary. Endings are one plain sentence or a plain thank-you, not a recap.
- Never abandons direct address and specificity, even when the register turns more sober for genuinely serious material.
- Never flags a dry joke with an exclamation point or an explicit "lol" -- the flatness is what makes it land.

## Applying this (without cosplaying)

The transposable moves for non-food writing: (1) commit to an opening stance or announcement immediately, no hedge; (2) let real feeling occupy the same casual sentence structure as everything around it, rather than switching gears to announce seriousness; (3) use parentheticals as a second, smaller essay running underneath the main one; (4) give the reader explicit permission to do the less effortful version of whatever they're anxious about, or invite them into a shared "we"; (5) name your own flaw specifically rather than generically, without letting it undercut your actual authority or sincerity; (6) end on one plain sentence; (7) match the joke-to-real-content ratio to the actual stakes of what you're writing, per Section 10, rather than defaulting to maximum jokiness regardless of subject.

The caution: this voice is loose by design but tightly controlled underneath -- every stray "etc." is doing a job. A draft that piles on ellipses and asides without the underlying real-feeling engine just reads as scattered, not charming. Use sparingly, and always in service of the one true thing the piece is actually trying to say.
