# Worked Example: Compensation-Data Vendor Pricing Discovery

Use this example when preparing or analyzing an initial vendor pricing call where the list price is known only partially, product fit is still being tested, and the buyer has several possible concessions. Source: [internal introductory call](https://app.notion.com/p/3c3ca4f355ac8157a6a9dafc95c56a32).

## Situation

A roughly 30-person company was evaluating a compensation-data vendor's paid tier for a largely discrete benchmarking exercise. The paid tier started at $9,750 annually for the 0–100 employee band. The buyer already had access to a useful free tier and had tested another provider, so the question was whether the incremental capabilities justified an annual commitment.

## Moves to Reuse

- **Address the decision hinge first.** The buyer explained that a five-figure annual purchase would be difficult to justify before completing the feature tour. The seller disclosed the starting price and discount levers early. Reuse: surface a gating term before investing the call in secondary details.
- **Reveal the mismatch without naming a ceiling.** The buyer described the company size, one-time exercise, and uneven ongoing need, but did not state a maximum budget. Reuse: give the seller a credible exception rationale without setting the negotiation floor.
- **Frame the paid tier as incremental value.** The buyer stated that the free tier handled much of the need. Reuse: compare the proposal with what the buyer can already accomplish, not with the vendor's broad category-level ROI story.
- **Use a real, non-bluffed BATNA.** The buyer mentioned evaluating another compensation-data product and tested the vendor's output against it. Reuse: create competitive pressure with genuine alternatives while withholding irrelevant detail.
- **Ask disconfirming questions.** The buyer asked where the vendor lacked country coverage, whether it suggested proxy countries, how deep the Japan data was, and how often new countries appeared. The call surfaced a real gap—Panama—and no proxy method. Reuse: ask for gaps and failure modes before accepting the curated demo.
- **Make the product prove itself.** The buyer tested a real senior software-engineer benchmark, changed the currency to USD, examined India data, and asked whether US-headquartered and locally headquartered comparison groups could be separated. Reuse: test the exact decision workflow before trading term for price.
- **Do the math live.** The buyer translated the 10% quick-sign discount to roughly $8,700 and reframed it as close to $1,000 per month, then checked annual billing mechanics. Reuse: convert seller framing into cash, cadence, and total exposure during the call.
- **Return to the actual use case after generic ROI framing.** When the seller argued that compensation is a large company expense, the buyer did not debate that premise; the buyer returned to whether this subscription was justified for the company's use. Reuse: acknowledge broad value while holding the incremental-purchase test.
- **Give the rep an internal exception case.** The buyer combined a small-customer mismatch with credible close signals: two-year potential, quick signature, signatory authority, and favorable-first-year economics. Reuse: make the request easy for the seller to repeat to finance.
- **Match non-cash value to a current vendor priority.** The buyer connected the company's AI reputation, sponsored writing, product feedback, and distribution to the vendor's forthcoming MCP/CLI. Reuse: offer strategic relevance, not generic exposure.
- **State authority and boundaries together.** The buyer could sign quickly, capped the term at two years, rejected two-year prepayment, and made any broader partnership subject to the appropriate internal owner. Reuse: pair credible authority with precise limits and approval ownership.
- **End with accountable follow-up.** The seller committed to send pricing options by end of day and to involve the partnerships team. Reuse: close with a written deliverable, named owner, and deadline.

## Concession Feedback from the Call

| Variable | Buyer's offer or limit | Vendor response | Reusable lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quick signature | Could sign quickly; the buyer is the signatory | The vendor associated quick signature with a 10% discount | Use credible speed only in exchange for defined value. |
| Contract term | Up to two years, with a materially favorable first year | The vendor identified multi-year as a discount lever | Trade term for explicit year-by-year economics, not a vague discount. |
| Payment timing | Offered net-5 | The seller said payment timing likely did not matter | Remove a concession once the vendor says it has no value. |
| Testimonial and logo | Willing to consider customer proof | The vendor identified this as a standard discount lever | Price standard proof separately from broader partnership assets. |
| Strategic partnership | Potential product feedback, sponsored writing, and MCP/CLI promotion, subject to internal approval | Required the vendor's partnerships team | Treat this as a conditional, separately negotiated package. |
| Prepayment | Annual upfront per year; no two-year prepayment | The vendor confirmed annual billing each year | Separate contract duration from cash timing. |

## Representative Language and Mechanism

Treat these as examples of construction, not scripts to copy verbatim.

- **Constraint without a ceiling:** "I'm not gonna be able to do that if it's like a five-figure annual contract." State what is structurally hard to approve without volunteering the acceptable price.
- **Conditional exchange:** "And if the give on our end needs to be multi-year, that's fine. But I'd love to see how favorable we could make year one." Put the give and required return in the same thought.
- **Credible close authority:** "I am the signatory on this." Reduce the seller's perceived execution risk only when true.
- **Plain hard limit:** "I'm not going to pay two years up front." State the boundary without padding it with a long defense.
- **Approval boundary:** "I'd have to talk to our head of partnerships about this." Explore additional value without accidentally granting it.
- **Conversational control:** "Let me just finish this up and then you can ask the question." Protect the framing when interrupted, then return the floor.

## Post-Proposal Counter: Price the Incremental Value

After the vendor sent written pricing, the buyer reconciled the proposal against the future-state workforce rather than using an approximate company headcount or the current HRIS count alone.

The operating roster, active HRIS records, planned rolloffs, and incoming hires produced:

- 29 expected team members
- 20 U.S. W-2 employees whose domestic benchmarking needs were covered through the vendor's free plan
- 9 international employees receiving the primary incremental value from the paid tier

At the vendor's $7,800 first-year price, the relevant unit cost was therefore $867 per international employee per year. A credible competing platform's $4,500 annual starting price provided an external calibration point.

The buyer countered with a two-year structure starting around $4,500 in year one, while leaving the vendor room to propose year-two pricing that reflected the longer commitment. The buyer was also open to paying slightly more for a one-year agreement in exchange for preserving flexibility.

### Moves to Reuse

- **Reconcile the contract-period population.** Combine the operating roster, vendor system, confirmed departures, and incoming hires. Do not subtract a current system count from an approximate future headcount.
- **Find the incremental paid-value denominator.** Subtract use cases already covered by a free tier, existing integration, or reduced-scope alternative before calculating per-unit value.
- **Treat contributed data as consideration.** When the customer supplies compensation, equity, usage, or benchmark data that improves the vendor's product, include that contribution in the economic exchange.
- **Use one decision-useful unit cost externally.** Keep broader scale comparisons and discount mechanics as internal support unless the vendor disputes the core premise.
- **Use a competitor as calibration, not a threat.** A credible alternative starting price makes the requested range easier for the seller to defend internally.
- **Structure the ask by term.** A longer commitment should earn a better opening price; a shorter term can reasonably carry a premium for flexibility.
- **Avoid over-specifying later years too early.** Anchor the first year and require the overall structure to reflect the commitment while leaving the rep room to find an approvable configuration.
- **Translate math into approval language.** "This is hard for me to defend" gives the rep a concise internal exception case.
- **Make close authority concrete.** When the buyer is the signatory, pair that fact with a real condition: "If we can arrive at something workable pricing-wise, I can sign immediately."

### Compression Rule for the Counter Email

The analysis can be expansive; the external email should usually contain only:

1. the future-state population,
2. the incremental paid-value population,
3. one effective unit-cost comparison,
4. one credible market anchor,
5. the term-sensitive ask, and
6. the conditional close.

## Do Not Copy Mechanically

- Do not offer a two-year term, fast signature, testimonial, or partnership unless each is accurate and authorized.
- Do not reuse a concession after learning that the vendor does not value it.
- Do not count roadmap capabilities such as a future MCP/CLI as present product value.
- Do not use rapport or humor as a substitute for math and proof.
- Do not stop at "send options." Once written pricing arrives, normalize each option and make a specific counter.
