# Worked Example: Compensation-Data Vendor Pricing Discovery

This generalized example shows how to prepare for or analyze an initial vendor pricing call when price and product fit are still being tested and the buyer has several possible concessions. Company, vendor, product, and commercial details have been generalized; use the mechanics, not the scenario as factual market data.

## Situation

A smaller company was evaluating a paid compensation-data tier for a focused benchmarking need. A free tier already covered much of the domestic use case, while the paid tier offered additional geographic coverage. The buyer had also evaluated an alternative provider. The decision was whether the paid tier's incremental capabilities justified an annual commitment.

## Moves to Reuse

- **Address the decision hinge first.** Explain when a high annual price would be difficult to justify before spending the call on secondary details. Ask for starting price and discount levers early.
- **Reveal the mismatch without naming a ceiling.** Describe the company's scale, the focused use case, and uneven ongoing need without volunteering the maximum acceptable price.
- **Frame the paid tier as incremental value.** Compare the proposal with what the free tier or another viable option already covers, not with the vendor's broad category-level ROI story.
- **Use a real, non-bluffed BATNA.** Test a credible alternative and use it as calibration rather than a threat.
- **Ask disconfirming questions.** Ask where coverage is missing, whether the vendor offers reasonable proxies, and how the data handles less common markets or roles. Surface gaps before accepting the curated demo.
- **Make the product prove itself.** Test a representative role and the exact comparison workflow the company needs before trading term for price.
- **Do the math live.** Translate discounts into actual cash, billing cadence, and total commitment. Keep first-year price distinct from multi-year total cost.
- **Return to the actual use case after generic ROI framing.** Acknowledge broad value, then return to whether this purchase is justified for the buyer's specific needs.
- **Give the rep an internal exception case.** Combine the economic mismatch with credible close signals and a clear explanation the account rep can take to its approvers.
- **Match non-cash value to a current vendor priority.** Product feedback, customer proof, or distribution may matter, but treat each as separate consideration and confirm the right internal owner approves it.
- **State authority and boundaries together.** Pair real signing authority with clear limits on term, payment timing, and any broader partnership offer.
- **End with accountable follow-up.** Close with the written deliverable, owner, and deadline.

## Concession Feedback

| Variable | Buyer position | Vendor response | Reusable lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Signature timing | Could move quickly if the economics worked | Faster signature could improve the offer | Trade speed only for defined value. |
| Contract term | Open to a longer term with favorable first-year economics | Longer term could affect discounting | Trade term for explicit year-by-year pricing. |
| Payment timing | Offered faster payment | Vendor placed little value on it | Stop offering a concession the vendor does not value. |
| Customer proof | Willing to consider a testimonial or logo use | Vendor identified proof as a possible discount lever | Price standard proof separately from broader partnership assets. |
| Strategic partnership | Open to exploring feedback or distribution | Required a separate internal approval path | Treat it as conditional and separately negotiated. |
| Prepayment | Preferred regular annual billing | Multi-year duration did not require multi-year prepayment | Separate contract duration from cash timing. |

## Representative Language and Mechanism

Treat these as examples of construction, not scripts to copy verbatim.

- **Constraint without a ceiling:** “A high annual commitment would be difficult for us to justify before we finish evaluating the product.” Name the approval constraint without volunteering an acceptable price.
- **Conditional exchange:** “If a longer term helps with pricing, we could consider it. I’d like to see the first-year economics before deciding.” Put the give and the required return in the same thought.
- **Credible close authority:** “I can sign this if we land on a structure that works.” Use only when accurate.
- **Plain hard limit:** “We can’t prepay the full term.” State the boundary without padding it with a long defense.
- **Approval boundary:** “I’d need to check with the right internal owner before offering that.” Explore additional value without accidentally granting it.
- **Conversational control:** “Let me finish this point, and then I’d like to hear your take.” Protect the framing when interrupted, then return the floor.

## Post-Proposal Counter: Price the Incremental Value

After written pricing arrives, reconcile the proposal against the expected population over the contract period. Use the operating roster, current systems, confirmed departures, and incoming hires rather than relying on an approximate headcount alone.

Then separate:

- the full population that may use the product;
- the use cases already covered by a free tier, existing integration, or reduced-scope alternative;
- the smaller group or workflow receiving the primary incremental value from the paid tier.

Calculate a decision-useful unit cost for that incremental group, and compare it with a credible alternative. Keep the exact math in the internal analysis; share only the detail needed to explain the counter. A longer term should earn a better opening price, while a shorter term can reasonably carry a premium for flexibility. Do not treat a multi-year term as multi-year prepayment unless the written proposal says so.

### Moves to Reuse

- **Reconcile the contract-period population.** Combine the operating roster, vendor system, confirmed departures, and incoming hires.
- **Find the incremental paid-value denominator.** Subtract use cases already covered by a free tier, existing integration, or reduced-scope alternative before calculating per-unit value.
- **Treat contributed data as consideration.** If the customer supplies data that improves the vendor's product, include that contribution in the economic exchange.
- **Use one decision-useful unit cost externally.** Keep broader scale comparisons and discount mechanics as internal support unless the vendor disputes the core premise.
- **Use a competitor as calibration, not a threat.** A credible alternative makes the requested range easier for the seller to defend internally.
- **Structure the ask by term.** A longer commitment should earn a better opening price; a shorter term can reasonably carry a premium for flexibility.
- **Avoid over-specifying later years too early.** Anchor the first year and require the overall structure to reflect the commitment while leaving room for an approvable configuration.
- **Translate math into approval language.** Explain plainly why the proposal is difficult to defend and what would make it workable.
- **Make close authority concrete.** Pair real signing authority with a condition: “If we can arrive at workable pricing, I can move quickly.”

### Compression Rule for the Counter Email

The analysis can be expansive; the external email should usually contain only:

1. the expected population;
2. the incremental paid-value group or workflow;
3. one useful unit-cost comparison;
4. one credible market anchor;
5. the term-sensitive ask; and
6. the conditional close.

### Generalized Email Example

> Hi [Seller name],
>
> Thanks for sending this over. I took a closer look at the pricing, and I don't think the current offer is quite in line with the incremental value we'd be getting.
>
> Most of our domestic benchmarking needs are already covered. What we're evaluating is the additional coverage for the use cases our current options do not handle.
>
> Could you send a revised option that reflects that narrower need, with first-year pricing and any later-year commitment shown separately? We're open to discussing term if the economics make sense, but we need to preserve annual billing and get approval for any broader partnership commitments.
>
> If we can arrive at a workable structure, I can move quickly on our side.
>
> Thanks,
> [Buyer name]

## Do Not Copy Mechanically

- Do not offer a longer term, fast signature, testimonial, or partnership unless each is accurate and authorized.
- Do not reuse a concession after learning that the vendor does not value it.
- Do not count roadmap capabilities as present product value.
- Do not use rapport or humor as a substitute for math and proof.
- Do not stop at “send options.” Once written pricing arrives, normalize each option and make a specific counter.
