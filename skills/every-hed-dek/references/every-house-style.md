# Every hed/dek house style

Use these as observed tendencies, not rigid templates. The analysis sampled 169 current hed/dek pairs across Chain of Thought, Working Overtime, Vibe Check, Source Code, Thesis, Guides, Context Window, Learning Curve, Also True for Humans, and The Crazy Ones, then checked representative pairs against the articles themselves.

## Core relationship

The hed creates a specific unresolved tension. The dek tells the reader what kind of resolution the article has earned.

The pair should align with the thesis, evidence, opening, and article shape:

- **Hed ↔ thesis:** Compress the article's real claim, transformation, concept, or result.
- **Dek ↔ evidence:** Name or imply the experiment, framework, reporting, historical story, personal experience, examples, or practical payoff that earns the hed.
- **Pair ↔ opening:** The first paragraphs should begin fulfilling the promise immediately.
- **Pair ↔ form:** A framework article promises a framework; a review gives a verdict and tradeoff; a reported story identifies its subject or access; an essay surfaces the idea it develops.

The pair must also be independently legible. Assume the reader has not yet learned the article's framework, scale, levels, coined vocabulary, or internal shorthand. A number or named concept can create curiosity, but the pair must either explain its plain-language significance or remain compelling without requiring the reader to decode it. The dek should reduce uncertainty, not introduce context that only the body can explain.

Common divisions of labor:

- Hed makes the claim; dek supplies the mechanism.
- Hed names a memorable concept; dek locates the problem it solves.
- Hed gives the surprising result; dek reveals what went wrong or why it matters.
- Hed dramatizes a collision; dek states the practical problem.
- Hed delivers a verdict; dek gives the evidence or tradeoff.
- Hed promises utility; dek defines the scope of the deliverable.

## Length

Observed medians:

- Hed: about 8 words and 44 characters. The middle half ran about 6–10 words and 32–54 characters.
- Dek: about 15 words and 86 characters. The middle half ran about 12–20 words and 66–105 characters.

These are useful pressure, not hard limits. Chain of Thought heds tend shorter and more conceptual. Vibe Check heds run longer because they carry a format label, product name, and verdict.

## Title case

Heds use headline-style title case, closest to AP style:

- Capitalize nouns, pronouns, adjectives, adverbs, and verbs, including short verbs such as “Is.”
- Capitalize prepositions of four or more letters: “With,” “From,” “Into,” “About.”
- Lowercase articles, coordinating conjunctions, short prepositions, and infinitive “to”: “a,” “an,” “the,” “and,” “but,” “or,” “at,” “by,” “for,” “in,” “of,” “on,” “to.”
- Capitalize the first and last word regardless of part of speech.
- Preserve product styling: ChatGPT, Claude Code, GPT-5.6, OpenAI, PowerPoint.
- Generally lowercase the second element of a compound modifier: Agent-native, AI-assisted, Science-based, Real-world, Four-hour, No-fluff.

Deks use sentence case: capitalize the first word, proper nouns, acronyms, and branded products.

## Punctuation

### Heds

- Omit a terminal period on an ordinary single-clause hed.
- A period can create a deliberate two-sentence turn: “AI Could Do Anything. Then It Met PowerPoint.”
- Questions are rare. Use one only when the article genuinely investigates an open question.
- A colon usually introduces a recurring format, named concept, comparison, or explanatory turn. Avoid stacking colons.
- Use an unspaced em dash for a consequential turn, collision, or qualification, not as automatic decoration.
- Parentheses are rare and should carry a genuinely useful aside.
- Avoid semicolons.

### Deks

- Prefer one sentence or a compact sentence fragment.
- Default to no terminal period for a single compact line. Use ordinary punctuation when the dek has multiple sentences, a question, a quote, or a deliberate staccato turn.
- Em dashes often hold the qualification omitted from the hed.
- Avoid semicolons and unnecessary parentheses.

## Interest engines

Create bounded curiosity: enough information to understand the territory, with the reasoning, mechanism, implications, or experience still to discover.

Effective engines include:

- **Confident, contestable claim:** “The Folder Is the Agent.”
- **Reversal:** the expected benefit creates an unexpected cost or limitation.
- **Confession with consequences:** the author's experience is itself evidence.
- **Named concept:** a memorable phrase the article defines and earns.
- **Specificity:** names, numbers, tools, prices, elapsed time, and concrete outcomes.
- **Narrow utility:** not “How to Use AI,” but a particular job, audience, or situation.
- **Verdict plus qualification:** especially for reviews; strength in the hed, limits in the dek.
- **Archetypal narrative:** a person, confrontation, or consequential decision cast in a vivid role.

Reveal the article's most compelling answer; withhold what makes that answer meaningful.

## Voice

- Opinionated, not pompous.
- Conversational, not sloppy.
- Intelligent and plainspoken.
- Excited about AI but clear-eyed about costs, limitations, and tradeoffs.
- Personal when personal stakes illuminate the argument.
- Wry rather than broadly jokey.
- Generous rather than contemptuous.
- Concrete rather than corporate.

The hed may be slightly more vivid or audacious. The dek should restore precision and trust.

## Format routing

| Format | Hed's job | Dek's job |
|---|---|---|
| Chain of Thought | Crystallize a concept or worldview | State the thesis or implication |
| Working Overtime | Name personal friction, experiment, or transformation | Connect experience to a broader work problem or takeaway |
| Vibe Check | Product name plus decisive verdict | State evidence, best use case, and important tradeoff |
| Source Code | Name a practice shift or memorable building principle | Show the workflow, result, or adoption path |
| Thesis | Make a market, product, or cultural argument | Name the mechanism, expert, framework, or stakes |
| Guide | Name the task or system directly | Specify deliverables, coverage, and reader outcome |
| Context Window | Offer a short thematic umbrella | Use “Plus:” to inventory the other important items |
| Learning Curve | Challenge an assumption about cognition or AI use | Restore nuance and human agency |
| The Crazy Ones | Cast a person in an archetypal historical role | Name the person and consequential event |
| Also True for Humans | Make an operational claim or describe an experiment | Supply the test, number, framework, or implication |

## Rejection tests

Reject a pair when it:

- overstates what the article proves;
- introduces an unsupported superlative;
- could sit on dozens of unrelated AI articles;
- repeats the hed in the dek;
- promises a framework, guide, test, or result that is absent;
- assumes an article-specific scale, level, coined term, or framework is universal knowledge;
- buries the recognizable noun or product name without editorial reason;
- asks a question the article has already answered;
- relies on vague futurism or generic intrigue;
- names an abstract trend without human stakes, evidence, or mechanism;
- becomes a list of keywords rather than a sentence;
- forces the same rhetorical construction into every option;
- uses first person when the writer's experience is incidental;
- sounds more certain, breathless, cynical, or cute than the article.
