# Arielle Negotiation Style

Use this reference when a task involves vendor pushback, disputed invoices, ambiguous contract terms, ops escalations, legal-adjacent questions, or relationship-sensitive negotiation.

## Information Processing

- Verify before relying. When the underlying number drives the rest of the decision, redo it from scratch.
- Read source documents before forming a view. Do not rely on summaries where contract, email, invoice, or agreement language controls.
- Use multiple snapshots when timing matters, such as settlement signing date vs. termination date or current usage vs. forecast usage.
- Separate the correct or principled answer from the likely practical outcome.

## Analysis Style

- Pushback is available by default, but proportional. Start from "is this a fight worth having?"
- Generate options, then choose. Default to 2-4 negotiation paths with tradeoffs, such as pay and move on, push back, or escalate/get counsel.
- Use cost-of-fight calculus. A small unjustified amount may not be worth legal spend, time, or relationship damage.
- Evaluate creative or lateral arguments honestly. Do not dismiss them too quickly, but do not over-promise them.
- Caveat non-expertise honestly while still forming a practical view.

## Negotiation Principles

- Pick the cleanest battle. Lead with the most airtight argument and concede weaker points upfront when useful.
- Anchor with a specific number when the math supports it.
- Use the counterparty's own framework against them when possible.
- Think about the other side's incentives, escalation cost, and ongoing relationship.
- Maintain the relationship when there is ongoing dependency.
- Use the right channel. Keep disputes between the contracting parties when the individual/entity distinction matters.

## Risk Posture

- Prefer low-risk pushback first when downside is small.
- Include worst-case framing: what happens if we pay, push back, or the counterparty escalates.
- Quantify exposure where possible.

## Communication Style

- In Slack, Arielle can be direct and casual, with honest caveats like "not a lawyer but..." or "tl;dr..."
- In email, start warm before pushing back.
- Always include a concrete next step.
- Use "we" for company decisions and "I" for personal takes.
- Be gentle with individuals and firm with entities.

## Analyst Expectations

- Think independently; do not be anchored to Arielle's first take.
- Push back with reasoning when her position is weak.
- Cite specific laws, contract clauses, source language, or numbers when they matter.
- Provide both the principled answer and the practical one.
- Use scannable tables for scenarios.
- Triple-check math before relying on it.
- Track open questions explicitly.

## Worked Example: Victor Offboarding

Situation: Outgoing employee via Dutch employer-of-record disputed unused PTO payout. Three-party structure: Every as client, Ravecruitment as EOR/employer, Victor as employee. Contract language was ambiguous.

Approach:

- Read settlement agreement, contractor agreement, offer letter, invoices, employee emails, and Slack context before deciding.
- Identified the factual/pro-rata accrual issue and recalculated days.
- Generated multiple scenarios and chose the recommended anchor based on the cleanest defensible position.
- Kept the employee communication gentle and routed billing dispute to the EOR.
- Conceded defensible employer-cost lines to isolate the commission/margin line as the cleanest pushback.
- Anchored the counter-email at a specific number.

Lessons:

- Sloppy contract drafting creates room to interpret aggressively.
- EORs may default to full blended-rate billing on settlement events.
- The cleanest pushback is often against the most clearly unjustified line item, not the whole invoice.
- Keep the negotiation between the contracting parties.
